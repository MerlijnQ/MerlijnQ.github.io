# Abstract
Medical data used in hospitals are often privacy sensitive and often cannot be shared with other institutions. However, with more AI applications being introduced to, for example, support radiologists in their work, training a model on as much high-quality data as possible can greatly contribute to its robustness and reliability. Decentralized training using federated learning methods is currently one of the most used techniques to still allow this. However, due to a high degree of heterogeneity in the data and models across institutions, convergence of a global, well-performing model can be challenging. In this work, we explore the algorithmic solutions proposed to mitigate model and different categories of statistical heterogeneity in the context of the medical domain. This demonstrates the need for a benchmarking framework that explores if these methods are effective against different kinds and degrees of statistical heterogeneity, which can be domain specific.

# Introduction

Personal devices have access to an unprecedented amount of data that can
be beneficial for training Machine Learning models. However, this data
is often privacy sensitive. Hence it cannot be logged to a central
database, To this end <a href="#ref-fedavg">[10]</a> introduced Federated Learning (FL), where
a model is trained according to a decentralized approach, which removes
the need to log data to a central database. Within their approach. they
select a specified number of clients per training round. All clients
store their own local database which they use to update their own local
model. After a few updates, the model weights of all clients are sent to
a central server where they are aggregated to get the global model,
which theoretically should perform better then the separate local
models. They call this strategy federated averaging (fedAvg). This
technique requires that all local clients start their independent
training procedure from a model with the same initialization in order to
prevent an arbitrary bad global model in non-convex settings, A key
challenge for federated optimization is that the training data available
per client can differ significantly and will not be representative of
the population distribution. This makes it non-independent and
identically distributed (non-IID), resulting in the local models
diverging too much away from each other, preventing the algorithm from
obtaining a well-performing converging global model.

Due to the importance of data privacy in medical settings, FL is also
widely used in this domain. Especially the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted
the need for international collaboration between clinical and scientific
communities which is why FL is a widely researched method in the field
<a href="#ref-dayan2021">[5]</a>, <a href="#ref-dynamicfusion">[12]</a>. In such a setting, typically
several hospitals work together by training models locally and combining
them in a model on a general server. These models can then be used for
problems such as clinical decision support or virus detection.
Heterogeneity, presented through, for example, the diversity in
equipment, preferences, and patients between these hospitals, is also a
major problem in this domain. Heterogeneity can highly affect the
performance of FL algorithms in such real-world scenarios
<a href="#ref-babar2024">[3]</a>.

Both vertical and horizontal FL suffer from the consequences of data
being non-IID as described by <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a>. The described fedAvg technique is
an example of horizontal FL <a href="#ref-haddadpour2019"">[7]</a>. Within this domain, the clients
share the same feature space but the sample space differs. In contrast,
in vertical FL <a href="#ref-heterofl"">[11]</a> clients share the same sample space, but
their feature space differs. Within such a system, there is no central
server training a global model. Rather, a guest client is selected that
aggregates the model outputs to calculate the final prediction error.
Using this, the intermediate gradients are calculated and send back to
the other clients to update their local model corresponding to the
feature space of the local data. Within the medical domain, vertical FL
can be useful if a patient uses multiple independent healthcare services
that cannot share the patient's data with each other.

Within the remainder of this survey, we will focus on the problem of
heterogeneity in horizontal FL. In this setting all clients have their
own patients, but they cannot share patient data with each other. An
example of a problem in which horizontal FL can be used is the decision
support system for the interpretation of medical imaging (e.g. through
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)).

## Search strategy
From the original paper on federated averaging <a href="#ref-fedavg">[10]</a> and a quick
search on the topic on google scholar, it became apparent that
heterogeneity is one of the main challenges in this field. From here we
decided to focus our survey on this topic and used keywords including
*Federated Learning* combined with *Heterogeneity*, *Model
heterogeneity* or *Medical* to search for articles on google scholar. We
read the abstracts of the papers in which the title indicated relevance
to our topic and considered whether it was published in an official
(peer-reviewed) venue or the number of times it was cited. After further
analysis of the seven W's and discussing the papers, we decided whether
to include the paper in our survey or not with respect to our topic and
the problem we wanted to address. Lastly, we took into account articles
cited by the articles we read as well, in addition to the search
strategy described above.

# Related Work

Within this section, we will discuss two types of heterogeneity.
*Statistical heterogeneity*, which is related to the data being non-IID
and *model heterogeneity*, where the model architecture between clients
can differ. Lastly, we study the overall impact on the medical domain.

## Statistical heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity, refers to a difference in local data
distributions between clients. This means it non-IID. This can cause
local models to severely diverge, which can negatively influence the
global parametric model as emphasized by <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a>. This survey defines
different categories of non-IID data in horizontal FL. Firstly, they
identify full overlapping attribute skew. Generally, this refers to
horizontally partitioned inconsistent data distributions among clients
due to attribute imbalance of the training data as a result of
perturbations. In the context of medical imaging, different clients can
make use of different scanning equipment that all introduce a different
degree of noise in the data. Simultaneously, feature imbalance can occur
due to differences between individuals (e.g. different people write
digits in different ways) or devices. Secondly, they identify label
skew. This refers to a difference in label size between clients (where
each client stores data belonging to different labels), or a label
distribution imbalance. In the context of medical imaging, the latter
can refer to some clients having a lot of data points corresponding to a
specific tumor and others having very little. Additionally, a preference
skew can be identified, where clients disagree on the label. Thirdly,
models depending on timestamped data can suffer from a temporal skew,
where data was recorded at different moments or different time scales
between clients. This can relate to, for example, predicting the growth
of a tumor over time. Lastly, quantity skew refers to a varying number
of training samples available between clients.

<a href="#ref-babar2024">[3]</a> investigated the impact of statistical
heterogeneity in FL for medical imaging through extensive experiments
across three datasets, comparing IID and non-IID environments. Previous
work has investigated this issue with a FL algorithm fedProx which we
will cover. However, previous work was done on pre-built datasets which
does not apply to the medical field. To assess this, 1) they trained
local models using CNN and MLP, 2) send local model information to the
server, 3) aggregate this local model information to form a global
model, 4) distribute global model to clients which can be used as a
starting point for the next round of local updating. This cycle is
repeated until satisfactory model performance is achieved or until a
predetermined number of rounds. The authors then vary the number of
clients, communication rounds and type of DL model used and measured the
resulting accuracies. Their experiments conclude that FL models struggle
to generalize in non-IID environments due to differences in sample sizes
between clients (quantity skew) and class imbalances (label skew). This
causes the global models to under perform, particularly on
underrepresented classes. This challenge persists regardless of the
number of communication rounds between client and server, increase in
the number of clients, or the use of different models. Limitations of
the study include the use of specific datasets which does not include
all forms of data heterogeneity in medical imaging, the use of FedAvg
which may not be applicable to all FL algorithms, and the
simulation-reality gap.

### Solutions

There are different strategies to handle these skews including data
sharing, continual learning and algorithm based approaches. We will
focus on the latter.

After the introduction of fedAvg, fedProx was introduced by <a href="#ref-fedprox">[9]</a> to
tackle the problem of system and statistical heterogeneity. System
heterogeneity refers to hardware constraints between different clients
as fedAvg does not allow variable amounts of local processing. Notably,
system and statistical heterogeneity are not independent of each other,
as a key insight of their work is a discovery of an interplay between
the two. To address these problems and prevent constraint devices from
not participating in the federated network while providing theoretical
convergence guarantees, the authors proposed to add a proximal term.
This allows every device to use a local solver $F_{k}(\cdot)$, with a
proximal term that penalizes the divergence from the original model, as
seen in Equation  <a href="#eq-fedprox">(1)</a>. Here, $\mu$ can be scaled dynamically with an
increase or decrease in the loss. This results in a more stable training
process under different degrees of system and statistical heterogeneity
compared to fedAvg. Within their tests, they updated the weights of the
global model proportional to the number of data points. This addresses
the aforementioned quantity skew.

<a id="eq-fedprox"></a>

$$
F_{k}(w) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w^t \rVert^2
$$

The importance of addressing statistical heterogeneity is also
emphasized by <a href="#ref-siloed-fl">[2]</a>, as this commonly occurs in histopathology
datasets. In their research, they assume a setting where all local
clients have enough quality data to produce effective local models with
full participation federated rounds. Batch normalization (BN) is often
found in deep federated networks. FedAvg would ignore the different
roles of its parameters and aggregated all each round. However, batch
statistics are running means and variances specific to the local
datasets, whereas the learned affine renormalization parameters can be
transferred across domains. By only sharing the renormalization
parameters across clients, this allows for local personalized tuning
where the intermediate activations are centered to a similar value
across clients, which is more robust against heterogeneity. This proved
to be an improvement over the fedAvg and FedProx algorithms. Future work
could quantify the privacy benefits of FedProx and investigate its
applicability.

<a href="#ref-bercea2022">[4]</a> argues that a robust FL system with unbalanced and privacy
sensitive datasets siloed across many institutes can support the
overburdened radiologist in interpretation and triage of acute findings
in MRI scans. They consider not averaging local statistics <a href="#ref-siloed-fl">[2]</a> not
reliable enough, as the BN parameters can still adapt to the dominating
client, hindering generalization. Therefore, to mitigate statistical
heterogeneity in brain anomaly detection using a proper unsupervised
domain-agnostic federated method to segment abnormalities in MR
findings, they proposed FedDis. To detect anomalies, they train an
autoencoder to compress and reconstruct an input. Abnormalities are
detected by the residual of the abnormal input and their healthy
reconstructed version. They disentangle the network to learn global
parameters representing common anatomical information and local
parameters representing appearance. This is enforced using a latent
contrastive loss and self-supervision. Only the global parameters are
shared between clients. This keeps appearance and contrast information
specific to each client, allowing them to use different scanners. This
accounts for the aforementioned attribute skew. This method outperforms
FedAvg, SiloBN and local client models in this task. Especially clients
with little data available benefit the most from this FL method. It is
not yet investigated how well this technique performs when more complex
architectures such as Gaussian mixture variational auto-encoders are
used nor the influence of training with different MR sequences obtained
in the same scan that should represent the same anatomical structure.
The latter represents a form of data heterogeneity that can severely
impact the performance of FL methods.

## Model heterogeneity

Besides statistical heterogeneity, <a href="#ref-huang2022">[8]</a> identified the
additional challenge of model heterogeneity. This is caused by
differences in model architectures between clients. This can be
preferable as different clients can have different design criteria,
distinct hardware capabilities, and intellectual property rights.

However, these models should still be combined to learn a generalizable
representation of the data without causing catastrophic forgetting.
Previous research attempted to achieve generalizable representations of
data using: 1) labeled data, which would require a server to collect
data with similar distributions to private data which is costly, 2) a
shared model, which raises computational cost and necessitates
additional model structure in participant side, or 3) group operation,
which uses unlabeled public data. For catastrophic forgetting, previous
research addressed this through hyperparameter configuration, which is
not a systematic solution to balance the knowledge provided by the local
clients.

### Solutions

To address this issue, <a href="#ref-huang2022">[8]</a> proposed Federated
Cross-Correlation and Continual Learning (FCCL). Their method uses
cross-correlation learning to encourage the invariance of some
dimensions and the diversity of others. The continual learning through
knowledge distillation provides soft and hard intra-domain information
throughout the continuous training process. Results show that FCCL
outperforms counterparts both inter- and intra-domain. This method
successfully enforces the described properties in both public and
private data. It is capable of balancing different knowledge from
multiple clients, reducing the chance of catastrophic forgetting. Future
work and limitations are not mentioned.

Another attempt to combat model heterogeneity in federated learning was
made by <a href="#ref-fang2022">[6]</a>. They identified that most FL algorithms assume
model homogeneity which is not applicable in many real-world scenarios.
Previous solutions for model heterogeneity leverage knowledge
distillation or unlabeled data for ensemble distillation. These methods
rely on a global consensus or shared models which means clients cannot
individually adjust their learning direction to accommodate the
differences among clients. This combined with issues stemming from
statistical heterogeneity also raise the problem of noisy feedback from
noisy clients.

To mitigate these issues, they aligning the knowledge distributions of
individual models on a public dataset. It prevents overfitting to local
noisy labels by weighing the contribution of each client according to
their loss and their learning efficiency. The difference between output
distributions of the model is then used for further training.
Furthermore, they promote learning of all classes by adopting Symmetric
Cross Entropy Learning (SL) loss.

Compared to state of the art HFL models, RHFL has a higher accuracy in
different noise settings, both in homogeneous and heterogeneous
scenarios.

Notably, the security guarantee of federated learning using model
distillation is limited as the local clients send their outputs to the
global server. An alternative approach is partial training, where sub
models are extracted from the global model and trained on the local
clients. <a href="#ref-fedrolex">[1]</a> illustrates that current implementations results in
uneven training of the global parameters. Therefore, they propose a
rolling sub-model extraction scheme. Every communication round, the
weights used by the local client are selected through a rolling window,
where the amount of selected trained weights are dependent on the client
their hardware constrained. All local updates per weight are averaged in
order to get the global parameters. This outperforms methods with random
extraction schemes for both small and large models and allows for the
training of bigger models. As the focus of the article is on the
training of the global model, it is not established which sub models to
extract for local clients upon deployment. Furthermore, a full
convergence analysis is not provided.

## Impact and application in Medical settings

Due to the prevalence of heterogeneous data in medical settings, global
FL models have difficulty converging and generalizing from diverse local
models <a href="#ref-babar2024">[3]</a>. However, in practice they still seem
to have an edge over local models trained on narrow data. A recent study
by <a href="#ref-dayan202">[5]</a> showed an increase in performance using such a
global FL model. They investigated the potential of federated learning
for predicting clinical outcomes in COVID-19 patients using a clinical
decision support model (EXAM). This model predicts mortality of infected
patients using clinical features. This implementation preserves the
security and privacy of data and leverages a larger dataset. Experiments
show that the global EXAM model is more robust and achieves better
results at individual sites than any model trained on only local data.
The authors provide possibilities for improvement in the form of
improved clinical information systems leading to a better network for
clients in FL, automated methods for model improvement, and the
investigation of the potential for a 'population drift' due to different
phases of disease progression.

Following the implementation of such FL algorithms, there is a need for
efficient communication between the clients and the global server.
<a href="#ref-dynamicfusion">[12]</a> identified massive communication costs of transferring
model updates.

To mitigate these costs, they propose a dynamic fusion-based FL approach
for COVID-19 case detection. This method allows clients to dynamically
fuse their model into the global model through a schedule based on their
training time. This results in better detection accuracy, fault
tolerance, and efficient communication compared to the default setting
of federated learning.

# Discussion

As <a href="#ref-dynamicfusion">[12]</a> establish, heterogeneity is one of the major
issues for federated learning in the medical field. Within this survey
we established that this is not only due to statistical heterogeneity
under different skews, but also model heterogeneity.

The investigated articles have their limitations. <a href="#ref-fedavg">[10]</a> noted that
they did not evaluate their method under changes in local datasets after
deployment and neither did the other models address this issue.
Furthermore, whilst  <a href="#ref-fedrolex">[1]</a>  states that their method is more privacy
robust than knowledge distillation approaches, the methods are rarely
evaluated regarding their security. Similarly, <a href="#ref-bercea2022">[4]</a>, <a href="#ref-siloed-fl">[2]</a> argue
is an important topic for future research as the purpose of federated
learning is preserving privacy of the training data across clients.
Simultaneously, our survey is limited to the algorithmic proposals to
tackle heterogeneity and does not dive into works that have evaluated
this explicitly.

Regarding the model heterogeneous setting, future works can explore how
to subtract local sub models <a href="#ref-fedrolex">[1]</a>  when a big global model is
trained. Notably, solutions like <a href="#ref-fang2022">[6]</a>  circumvent this by
not training a global model at all but only focus on joint training of
the local models.

The solutions discussed evaluate the overall performance of the FL model
with a mention of training on non-IID data. However, the robustness of
the algorithmic contributions against the different categories of
non-IID data defined by <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a> is never robustly evaluated. If a
category is identified, evaluation of a technique is often restricted to
one (e.g. <a href="#ref-bercea2022">[4]</a>). This makes it more challenging to evaluate in which
settings the techniques can be applied most efficiently when it is known
what kind of skew is most prominent within the FL system. Furthermore,
this can help guide the direction of research to make these systems more
robust. Therefore, we identify the need for a proper benchmark on which
these techniques can be tested. We acknowledge that this can be
challenging due to differences between domains and the privacy issue
that is addressed by FL in the first place.

Finally, there is no clear methodological setup based on real world data
found within the studies we investigated. Studies use varying number of
clients, architectures and levels of heterogeneity. For example
<a href="#ref-fedrolex">[1]</a> experiments with up to 200 clients using a similar
architecture with different number of kernels or nodes, while
<a href="#ref-fang2022">[6]</a> experiments with 4 clients, all with different
architectures. Additionally, whilst the most common metric to measure
performance is accuracy over communication rounds, papers deviate from
this as well. This makes it difficult to compare models between studies
and it is unclear whether such methods are applicable in the real world.
Along with results found in simulation studies such as
<a href="#ref-babar2024">[3]</a>, a gap between simulation and reality appears
within still needs to be addressed.
